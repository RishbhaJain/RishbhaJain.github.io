Project 1: Colorizing the Prokudin-Gorskii Collection

Each scan contains three grayscale photographs of the same scene, taken through blue, green, and red filters. Stacking them directly produces colored outlines because the exposures are slightly displaced. I aligned the channels first, then worked on the borders and color.

The results below cover the 14 supplied scans and three additional scans from the Library of Congress. All TIFFs were processed at their original resolution. The images on this page are smaller copies for display.

1. Single-scale alignment

I converted the input to floating point in [0, 1] and split it into three equally sized sections in B, G, R order. Blue is the reference. Green and red are shifted independently, then stacked in RGB order.

For the small JPEGs, I tested every integer shift from −15 to 15 in both directions: 961 candidates per channel. The score is the sum of squared differences over an interior region. Minimizing this gives the same shift as minimizing the L2 distance, since the square root does not change the ranking.

SSD(dy, dx) = Σ [A(y − dy, x − dx) − B(y, x)]²

The scoring margin is 10% of the smaller image dimension, with a minimum of 15 pixels. This keeps the scan borders and wrapped pixels out of the comparison. Every candidate uses the same number of pixels.

Offset convention: the tables use (x, y): positive x moves right and positive y moves down. The code and CSV files store (dy, dx), so their order is reversed here.

2. Image pyramids

A 15-pixel search is too small for the full-resolution scans. Increasing the window at full resolution would make every candidate expensive, so I used a coarse-to-fine search.

  1. Repeatedly downsample each channel by a factor of two until its longest side is at most 300 pixels.
  2. Search ±15 pixels at the coarsest level.
  3. Double the estimated shift at the next level and search ±3 pixels around it.
  4. Continue to the original resolution and apply the final integer shift.

The pyramid construction and search are implemented directly. Scikit-image supplies the anti-aliased resize, not an alignment or pyramid routine. The settings are the same for every image.

For this search I used normalized cross-correlation (NCC). Each candidate patch is centered by subtracting its mean, then normalized by its length. A higher score means better agreement.

NCC(A, B) = Σ[(A − Ā)(B − B̄)] / √(Σ(A − Ā)² Σ(B − B̄)²)

I exclude 10% along each side when scoring and compare only the common overlap of the shifted interiors. The reference baseline uses the original grayscale channel intensities. The edge-based version below uses the same search and NCC score.

3. Bells and whistles

Aligning edges

The same object can have very different brightness in different color channels. Emir is the clearest example: matching raw intensity sends the red channel to the wrong location. I computed horizontal and vertical Sobel derivatives and used their magnitude, √(Gx² + Gy²), as the matching image. Edges are computed separately at each pyramid level after downsampling. The resulting shifts are applied to the original channels.

Brightness NCC gives G (24, 49) and R (-250, 96). Edge NCC gives G (24, 49) and R (40, 107). The robe and face line up much better with the edge score. The edge maps are only used to estimate displacement; they are not part of the reconstructed color image.

Automatic cropping

The first crop is the valid overlap of all three shifted plates, which removes pixels wrapped by np.roll. The remaining border is detected from the image. Long transitions reinforce one another when signed Sobel derivatives are averaged along rows or columns; small scene edges tend to cancel.

I also sample up to 192 lines on each side and check each color channel independently. A colored fringe can be a dark band in just one channel. A candidate band needs agreement on at least 65% of the sampled lines and must connect to the outside. The first candidate must be within 3.5% of the edge; derivative groups are searched within 6%, and confirmed border material can be followed within 8%. These are search limits, not fixed crop margins.

Wharf exposed a weakness in checking only for near-black pixels: its bottom border is uneven dark gray. As a fallback, a pixel counts as dark when it is below 35% of the nearby interior brightness and at least 0.08 darker on the [0, 1] scale. Confirmed black or white strips use 80% intensity recovery; the gray-band fallback uses 95% so a faint tail is not left behind. A clear step into the photograph stops the search early.

The boundary is the 85th percentile of the recovery positions across valid sample lines. I intersect the channel bounds to obtain one rectangle. On wharf the final crop is rows 238:3073 and columns 188:3500, with the ending indices excluded.

This removes the broad colored strips while keeping the mast and dock. The detector is still conservative: local stains and damaged corners can remain, and a rectangular crop cannot follow every irregular edge.

White balance

I used a white-patch estimate after cropping, so the bright scan border does not influence it. For each channel, its 99th percentile estimates the white level. The target is the mean of those three estimates; each channel is multiplied by the target divided by its own estimate.

wc = percentile99(Ic),   gainc = mean(wR, wG, wB) / wc

Church receives RGB gains of (1.071, 1.104, 0.862). This reduces its blue cast. Using a percentile is less sensitive to an isolated bright pixel than using the maximum. It still assumes the bright parts provide a useful white reference; a strongly colored highlight can give the wrong estimate.

Contrast

I map the shared 1st and 99th intensity percentiles to 0 and 1 and clip values outside that interval. The same lower and upper limits apply to all RGB channels. This spreads the occupied intensity range without separately stretching the channels.

I′ = clip((I − p1) / (p99 − p1), 0, 1)

For melons the limits are 0.102 and 0.937. The fruit and shaded stall become easier to distinguish. The tradeoff is that the brightest and darkest tails are clipped; detail that is already missing in the scan cannot be recovered.

Color mapping

I tried a small RGB mixing transform after contrast stretching. Let Y = 0.2126R + 0.7152G + 0.0722B. The mapping moves each color 8% farther from its estimated gray level:

RGB′ = clip(Y + 1.08(RGB − Y), 0, 1)

This is equivalent to a 3 × 3 matrix with off-diagonal terms. Its rows sum to one, so a neutral gray stays neutral before clipping. It makes the color separation slightly stronger without applying a different manual adjustment to each photograph.

The change is modest and mainly increases saturation. This is an experimental rendering choice, not a calibration of the original filters. It does not estimate the illuminant, so white balance remains a separate step. Saturated colors can clip.

6. Runtime and limitations

The full run took between 0.96 and 16.23 seconds per input, with a median of 12.65 seconds across all 17 scans. The total for each input includes loading, shared pyramid construction, both alignment methods, the four enhancement stages, JPEG writing, and the individual comparisons. JPEG inputs also include the single-scale run. It excludes the final combined galleries and this report.

Emir is the clear global failure of the brightness baseline. Edge matching fixes the large displacement there, but neither metric can account for a person moving between exposures. The tea-workers image is a useful example: the background aligns while some faces remain doubled. Rotation, scale changes, lens distortion, scratches, and uneven exposure also fall outside the translation model.

The enhancements are not equally helpful on every image. Cropping removes most broad borders but leaves some isolated damage. White-patch balance depends on the scene containing a reasonable bright reference, and contrast can exaggerate washed-out highlights. The color matrix produces a small visual change rather than recovering measured historical colors.
